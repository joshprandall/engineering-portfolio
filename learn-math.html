GUIDED LESSON / DISCRETE MATHEMATICS

Translate, negate, and test a quantified claim

Prerequisite: predicates and a specified domain. Outcome: distinguish a proof of “for every” from a witness for “there exists.”

Let the domain be the integers and let P(x) mean “x² ≥ x.” The statement ∀x P(x) is false: x = −1 gives 1 ≥ −1, but x = 1/2 is not an integer, so it cannot refute this particular claim. In fact, x² − x = x(x − 1) is nonnegative for every integer x, so the statement is true over the stated domain. Change the domain to real numbers and x = 1/2 becomes a counterexample because 1/4 < 1/2. A domain is part of the assertion, even when the prose leaves it implicit.

Quantifier order and negation
ClaimMeaningNegation
∀x ∃y R(x,y)Each x may have its own y∃x ∀y ¬R(x,y)
∃y ∀x R(x,y)One y works for every x∀y ∃x ¬R(x,y)

Worked example

Over integers, ∀x ∃y (y > x) is true: choose y = x + 1 after x is specified. Reversing the quantifiers gives ∃y ∀x (y > x), which is false: for any proposed y, choose x = y. The witness for the first claim may depend on x; the reversed claim requires one fixed witness.

Try it, then check

Negate “For every real x there exists a real y such that y² > x.” State whether the original assertion is true and exhibit an appropriate y in terms of x.

Reveal an explained solution

The negation is “There exists a real x such that for every real y, y² ≤ x.” The original is true: choose y = |x| + 1. Then y² > x. For x ≥ 0, (x + 1)² > x; for x < 0, any nonzero square exceeds x. The proposed negation is false.

Common mistake: negating only the predicate and leaving the quantifiers intact. Apply ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x) and ¬∃y Q(y) ≡ ∀y ¬Q(y), one quantifier at a time.

Limit: checking many samples is evidence for a conjecture, never a proof of an infinite universal claim. A single valid counterexample does refute it. A finite-domain enumeration can be exhaustive when the domain is explicitly finite.
